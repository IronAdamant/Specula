## Table J7-1. Maximum-speed scaling model (read directly, preprint)

Source: Hirt MR, Jetz W, Rall BC, Brose U, "Universal scaling of maximum speed with body mass - Why the largest animals are not the fastest", bioRxiv preprint 10.1101/095018, posted 17 Dec 2016, https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/095018v1.full. Published version: *Nature Ecology & Evolution* (2017), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-017-0241-4 (not opened in this pass).

Model form: a power law a·M^b times a limiting term between 0 and 1 that falls with mass through the exponent i. The equations are images on the preprint page and were not read; the commonly cited form v_max = a·M^b·(1 − e^(−h·M^i)) should be checked against the paper before use. Speed first rises with mass as a power law, then falls for large animals because they run out of fast-available energy before they finish accelerating. Data: 617 data points for 470 species, 5.7×10⁻⁸ to 108,000 kg, running, flying and swimming; overall fit R² = 0.893.

| Mode | a | b | i | n (data points, figure 2 legend) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Running | 26 | 0.24 | 0.6 | 453 |
| Flying | 144 | 0.27 | 0.72 | 55 |
| Swimming | not given in the text | 0.36 | 0.56 | 109 |

The text says "flying animals are nearly six times faster than running ones (a = 144 and 26)". The remaining constant (h), the swimming a and the units are in Supplementary Table 4 (per the text, 'parameter values in Supplementary Table 4'), which was not read, so this table cannot yet be turned into speeds without that table. Do not use it for numbers until h is filled in from the supplement.

Other findings in the same text: in running and flying animals, warm-blooded species tend to be faster than cold-blooded ones of the same size; in water the reverse holds.

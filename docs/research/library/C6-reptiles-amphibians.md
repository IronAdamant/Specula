## Sources disagree

1. **Tortoise duty factor.**
   - *T. graeca*: 0.78 ± 0.01, with no speed effect (Ewart 2022).
   - *T. hermanni*: 56.8–87.1%, and the slowest individual had the *lowest* DF, 69.1% (Schmidt 2016).
   - Giant tortoise: 80.5 ± 0.58% (Zani 2005).
   - Schmidt explains the reversal by the large animal's slow swing. Don't drive turtle DF from speed alone.
2. **Frog take-off angle, *Rana/Lithobates pipiens*.**
   - Near-maximal jumps average 54 ± 1° (Moo 2017, 2 frogs).
   - Porro 2017 Table 4 quotes a mean of 26° (range 16–42°) for the same species.
   - The difference is probably the selection of maximal vs. all jumps. Use 54° only for "max-distance" leaps.
3. **Salamander walking DF.** *Dicamptodon* 72% (Ashley-Ross 1994a), *Salamandra* about 74% (Pierce 2020), *Pleurodeles* about 77% (Karakasiliotis 2013). This is a spread across species, not an error. Use 0.72–0.77.
4. **Axial wave at speed.**
   - Salamander walking is a standing wave in all three salamander sources.
   - At a trot, Ashley-Ross 1994a describes standing and travelling patterns blending.
   - Ritter 1992 (unverified) says lizards switch to a travelling wave as speed rises.
   - The *direction* agrees; there is no exact switch speed in any open source.
5. **Crocodile top speed.**
   - Hutchinson 2019 says nothing above 5 m/s has been recorded, and that Zug's "18 m/s" is an error for about 1.8 m/s.
   - Popular figures above 5 m/s should not be used.
6. **Licence metadata, Mazouchova 2010.** Resolved 2026-11-17 per Library Verify: the original licence is **CC BY 2.0** (PMC/OAI). Crossref's CC BY 4.0 cannot be original (4.0 dates from 2013); the Royal Society copyright wording on the PDF sits alongside the CC BY 2.0 grant.
7a. **Zani 2005 external work.** The Discussion prints 0.42 ± 0.083 J kg⁻¹ m⁻¹; elsewhere the paper gives 0.41 ± 0.092 (row 4.8 uses the latter). Flagged by Library Verify 2026-11-17.

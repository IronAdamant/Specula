## J13-3. Thunder (read directly)

| Quantity | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | channel heats the air very fast; the air expands then contracts; the initial shock decays to ordinary sound "within ten yards or so" | NOAA NSSL; NWS "Thunder" page |
| How far it is heard | about 10 miles (about 16 km) | NWS "Understanding Lightning: Thunder" and NWS overview page |
| How far it is heard, upper | up to 25 miles (about 40 km) | NOAA NSSL "Lightning Basics" |
| Sound by distance | very close: a tearing sound (high frequencies) from the nearest channel; a little farther: a sharp click or loud crack; tens of seconds later: low rumbling from the far parts of the flash, because air absorbs high frequencies faster | NOAA NSSL; NWS "Thunder" adds that a rumble means at least several miles away and the loud boom is the main channel reaching the ground |
| Counting rule | sound travels about 1 mile per 5 seconds, or 1 km per 3 seconds | NWS; NSSL |

## Sources disagree

- Channel temperature: NOAA (NWS, NSSL) about 50,000 °F (about 27,800 °C); Wikipedia "Lightning" about 30,000 °C, citing a different NWS page, "How Hot Is Lightning?", https://weather.gov/safety/lightning-temperature (ref name DepCom2025), not the NWS pages this file uses; HyperPhysics 15,000-60,000 °F. Use about 30,000 K as the round figure and the HyperPhysics range for variation.
- Typical peak current: HyperPhysics "up to 10 kiloamperes" from Williams and "most measurements 5,000 to 20,000 amps" from Uman; Wikipedia 30 kA for an average negative flash. The 30 kA figure is the usual engineering median; treat 5-20 kA as the lower body of the spread.
- Flash duration: about 0.2 s (HyperPhysics) against a median of 0.52 s (Kákona 2023 via Wikipedia).
- How far thunder carries: 10 miles (NWS) against up to 25 miles (NSSL).

## Not found

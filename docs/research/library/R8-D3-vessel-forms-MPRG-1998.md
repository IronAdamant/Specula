## 2. How the guide classifies

**Basic form plus component parts (1.3.3, p7).** The premise is that a small number of basic shapes exist and that
rims, handles, spouts, feet and the like are added to them. Added parts may change what the vessel is used for but,
the guide holds, do not change its basic shape: a hemispherical bowl stays hemispherical with or without a handle.
The three basic shapes (p7):

| Basic class | Section | Test (restated) |
|---|---|---|
| Jugs, bottles, flasks | 3 | Closed and tall: both the mouth and the maximum girth are narrower than the height |
| Jars | 4 | Closed, girth about equal to height, mouth narrower than both |
| Bowls and dishes | 5 | Open: mouth wider than both the girth and the height |

Rare exceptions are admitted (p7). Forms merge into one another and the user decides by local knowledge; the same
decision applies to whole profiles and to sherds (p8).

**Two systems, profile and proportion (2.3.1, pp9-10).** Each basic form is classified by *profile* (the silhouette;
each named profile has its own sheet) and by *proportion* (a ratio of height to widths). Size and capacity are
explicitly secondary: the same profile at another size is the same form (1.3.2, p7). Bowls get a third axis, depth
(shallow, medium, deep; p9).

**Which measurements define proportion (2.2, p9).** The user is told to establish the "ratio of height to rim to
maximum diameter". In practice the proportion sheets use height and maximum diameter (jugs, jars), and height, rim
diameter and base diameter (bowls, dishes, plates, cups, mugs). The base enters through "rim larger than base" tests
for open forms and in Appendix I for jars. Maximum diameter is used for closed forms; for open forms the rim is
normally the widest part.

**Adapted forms (2.3.2, p10).** Handles, spouts, feet, pedestals, piercing, division and multiplication make named
variants (handled jar, pipkin, spouted bowl, colander, divided dish). Variants keep the profile name; the guide's own
sample name is "Convex-sided Spouted Bowl" (p10). Splitting further by handle type is left to local users.

**Drawing conventions (2.4.2, p11).** The standard drawing is stylised and not to scale; bases are drawn flat unless
the base defines the form (ginger jar, hemispherical bowl); rims are simplified; wall thickness is drawn the same
everywhere except where thickness defines the form (mercury jar). Thickness is therefore not part of the system.

**What it leaves out (1.4, pp7-8).** Function (the guide names forms, not uses; it warns that "cooking pot" implies
use, p33), date, source, fabric, most regional variants, many continental and factory wares, tin-glazed earthenware and
stoneware forms; building materials, kiln furniture, moulds, loom weights, spindle whorls; objects re-used after firing.

**Its own stated limits (1.5, p8).** It is "a guide", not a final answer; the compilers chose ease of use over strict
logic; the order is admitted to be imperfect (amphorae and the mercury jar sit in section 10, p9); drawings are a
stylised classic with variation either side. Section 10 is called the most incomplete part (p9).

## 3. The proportion rules

"Stated" means the text gives the relation outright. "My reading" means I inferred the inequality from prose. Where
the book says "equal" it means roughly equal; no tolerance is ever given.

| Jar family | class test | m about h; r < m and r < h | 7 | stated |
| Open (bowl, dish family) | class test | r > m and r > h | 7 | stated |

Tally: 21 rows are "stated" (including qualitative and overlap rows; the numeric ones are the three class tests, jug
x3, jar x3, bowl, dish, plate, beaker, cup, mug), 5 are "my reading" (jug/jar boundary, bottle, three bowl depths),
4 have no numeric rule (flask, frying pan, lid, section 10 forms).

**The bowl depth line is garbled as printed.** The depth sheet (p58) words shallow as "maximum diameter between
one-third and two-thirds of height", medium as two-thirds to equal, and deep as equal. Read literally this describes
tall vessels and contradicts the class definition on p56 (height at least a third of the rim diameter). My reading
swaps the two quantities, so the depth bands compare height with diameter. It is an inference; the drawings, which
were not seen, would settle it.

**Gaps and ties.** (1) Bowl and dish share the boundary h = r/3 (bowls "one-third or more", dishes "between one-third
and one-seventh"). (2) The dish band reaches down to one seventh; plates (below one fifth, everted) overlap the dish
band between 1/7 and 1/5. (3) Cup (r > h) and bowl (h >= r/3) overlap for small handled vessels; the guide itself says
small handled bowls fall within cups (pp69, 71). (4) No sheet says how r relates to m for jugs beyond both being
smaller than h. (5) The jug and jar rules use m only; bowls, dishes, plates and cups use r and h.

### 6.2 Lighting and heating (section 8, pp104-121), defining characteristics only

- **Candlestick:** a portable stand with a socket for a candle. Saucer type: socket centred in a drip tray, maybe a
  loop handle to the saucer rim (p105). Upright type: socket above a drip tray on a usually hollow pedestal, maybe a
  loop handle and extra drip trays (p105).
- **Lamp:** an oil reservoir. Compartmented: deep square or rectangular slab with two or more cup-shaped holders cut
  in the top (p107). Double-shelled: pedestal lamp plus drip tray (p107). Hemispherical: small bowl with a pinched
  wick lip, also usable as a crucible (p108). Pedestal: small bowl on a pedestal, wick lip or lug handle (p108).
  Saucer: bowl on pedestal above a drip tray, maybe pierced suspension lugs (p109). Spike: small bowl with a solid
  pointed base (p109).
- **Lantern:** closed, nearly cylindrical, one or more cut-out windows, a tubular candle socket in the base, a loop
  handle opposite the window; rare, copies metal (p110).
- **Others:** warming pan (squat closed body, narrow neck, many holes, one long straight handle, p111); curfew
  (inverted bowl with a loop handle and holes; flared, hemispherical, half-round and flanged forms, pp112-117);
  chafing dish (bowl or dish on a hollow pedestal or tripod with equally spaced rim supports and vents, p118);
  fuming pot (jar shape with cut-outs on a pedestal, p119); heating stand (narrow-necked, pierced, on a pedestal, may be
  angular, p119); beverage warmer (small handled stepped open vessel sitting in a larger one, p120); Dutch oven
  (semicircular or rectangular, L-shaped profile, feet, handles at the back, maybe two tiers, p121).

## 7. Appendix I: a worked example of finer classification (pp210-212)

The guide shows how one profile of one form could be subdivided, using the rounded jar. Three characteristics are
combined: (1) rim or neck width against base width (smaller, equal, larger); (2) the height of the point of maximum
diameter (upper half, centre, bottom half); (3) overall height as squat, medium or tall, given as a ratio of the
widest horizontal size to the vertical size. Size is deliberately not used. Examples were found for every cell of
the grid for the rounded jar only, not for other jar profiles. The cut-offs between squat, medium and tall are not in
the text layer (they may be in the drawings); the jar band at p35 is the nearest statement. The grid is 3 x 3 x 3 = 27
cells.

## 8. What the guide does not give

I searched for cm, mm, litre, capacity, pint, gallon and "diameter of". Findings:

- **No absolute dimension anywhere** in the form sections. The only hits are the words "capacity" or "size" used to
  say that size is secondary (pp7, 13, 33, 56), "large capacity" for tankards (p90), "small capacity" for ointment
  jars (p39), "diameter of the rim" in a handle definition (p178), and a wheel-stamp diameter in the decoration
  glossary (p194), which is not about vessel form.
- Real-example drawings are said to be mostly 1:6 (p11); the text layer carries no scale numbers.
- **Numeric rules are only the ratio rules in section 3.** The only angle figures are the 90-degree thresholds for the
  base angle (p174) and "approximately 45 degrees" for a side-handled urinal's handle (p166).
- **Not given:** wall thickness (standardised on purpose, p11), curve geometry (how convex, how deep a waist, how
  strong a shoulder), neck height or width, rim width ratios, handle size and attachment height, foot and pedestal
  height, lid overhang, volumes.

A shape library would still have to estimate on its own: absolute scale (a mug versus a jug at the same ratio); neck
proportions; where the maximum diameter sits in each profile (stated only for pear-shaped: lower half; rounded jug:
upper half near the middle; biconical and carinated: at the join or carination); curvature; wall thickness; base
diameter for closed forms (only "smaller than m" for jars); handle, spout and foot sizes. These would be the
engine's own estimates and should be labelled so.

## 9. Open points

1. **The bowl depth sheet (p58) contradicts itself as printed**; see section 3. My reading may be off in detail
   (for example whether deep means h = diameter or h >= diameter).
2. **Bowl and dish tie at h = r/3**; the plate band (below r/5) overlaps the dish band (down to r/7).
3. **Jar medium is "m equal to h"** with no tolerance; the book's real examples are only roughly so.
4. **Jug versus jar has no tolerance:** a jug needs h > m, jar medium is m = h and wide is m > h, so the boundary is
   one value; the guide leans on neck shape for borderline cases (pp12, 33).
5. **Class test versus sheet:** 1.3.3 (p7) gives the jug class r < h and m < h; the jug proportion sheet (p15) uses
   only m. No section says how r relates to m for a jug.
6. **"Waisted" is used differently in drinking vessels** than elsewhere in the guide (p80), and Appendix I's
   rim-versus-base cells are shown by drawings only.
7. **Terms used but not defined in the text:** kicked and pointed bases (p173), cordoning (p187, explicitly not
   described), the "stepped" profile of the beverage warmer (p120), the lobed lug shape (p183), the Hurst flask types
   (references only).
8. **Cup versus bowl:** small handled bowls "may fall within" cups (pp69, 71) while cups need r > h and r > b: a
   handled bowl with h < r satisfies both; no rule chooses.
9. **Beaker rule versus borrowed profiles:** beakers are "generally" taller than wide (p81) yet borrow bowl profiles,
   where r > h; and small hemispherical bowls "may be called beakers" (p62).
10. **Flared-jar squatness** is judged against the "minimum diameter" (p40), a different test from the jar bands
    (which use m and h).
11. **Page citations in section 6.3** are the pages where each sheet begins in the text layer; a sheet may spill onto
    the next page.

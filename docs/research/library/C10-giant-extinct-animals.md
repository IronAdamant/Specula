# C10: Giant extinct animals: pterosaurs, sauropods and Tyrannosaurus, size, mass and speed estimates

Researched 2026-11-17 (all URLs accessed 2026-11-17). Brief section 3, "Fantasy creatures: pterosaur and large dinosaur reconstructions with mass estimates and locomotion arguments". Living-animal body plans, gaits and birds are in the main library's C1 to C9. Skeletal reconstruction images were not gathered this round (see Not found).

All rows below are read from Wikipedia text and name the paper each estimate comes from; the papers were not opened unless stated.

Locomotion argument (Witton and Habib 2010 abstract, read via Europe PMC 2026-11-17): bird and pterosaur launch kinematics are too different to be interchangeable; bone strength and flap-gliding performance show giant pterosaurs could fly; 10-11 m spans and 200-250 kg are the most reliable upper estimates, against earlier claims of 13 m and 544 kg.

## C10-2. Sauropod: *Argentinosaurus*

| Estimate | Method | Source (as cited) |
|---|---|---|
| 80-100 t | comparison | Paul 1994, *The Dinosaur Report* pp. 12-13 |
| 83.2 t | volume of the Museo Carmen Funes skeleton reconstruction | Sellers et al. 2013, "March of the titans", *PLoS ONE* doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0078733 |
| 90 and 95 t | limb-bone scaling (questioned for very large error) | Benson et al. 2014, *PLoS Biol.* doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1001853, and 2018 |

Sellers et al. 2013 also simulated gait with a musculoskeletal model. Their text states that the maximum speed obtained is equivalent, in dimensionless (Froude) terms, to a human with 0.9 m leg length walking at 1 m/s, using a diagonal gait with lateral couplets (Discussion, read 2026-11-17 at https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0078733). The absolute speed is in their results tables, not retyped here.

## Sources disagree

- *Quetzalcoatlus* mass spans 30 to 440 kg across authors, driven mostly by assumed wingspan and bone density.
- *Argentinosaurus*: 83 t (volumetric) against 90-95 t (scaling).
- *T. rex* top speed: 4.5 to 20 m/s across studies.
